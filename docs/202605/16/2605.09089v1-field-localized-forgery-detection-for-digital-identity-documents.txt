Abstract. Digital identity verification systems used in remote onboard-ing rely on document images to authenticate users, making them vulner-able to localized manipulations of key identity fields such as facial pho-tographs and textual information. Existing forgery detection methods, developed primarily for natural-image forensics, show limited transfer-ability to structured identity documents. We propose FLiD, a lightweight field-localized framework that targets critical identity regions rather than processing full-document images. A fine-tuned object detector first local-izes face and text fields; a frozen MobileNetV3-Small backbone then ex-tracts compact field-level embeddings, which are classified by lightweight neural network with only 191K trainable parameters. FLiD achieves AUC scores of 0.880 (face), 0.954 (text), and 0.923 (both-field attacks), with corresponding EERs of 18.05%, 11.61%, and 15.16%, representing abso-lute reductions of 29–35 percentage points over a full-document baseline trained from scratch. FLiD also consistently outperforms general-purpose manipulation detectors (TruFor, MMFusion, UniVAD) across all attack scenarios while requiring 13 × fewer parameters and 21 × fewer FLOPs. 

Keywords: Digital Identity Verification · Identity Document Forgery Detection · KYC Systems 

# 1 Introduction 

Forgery detection in identity documents has become a critical challenge in mod-ern digital identity verification systems, where banks, fintech platforms, telecom providers, and other third-party services routinely require users to upload pho-tographs or scanned copies of identity documents for remote authentication. The reliance on such remote onboarding workflows has increased significantly follow-ing the COVID-19 pandemic, during which physical verification procedures and contact-based biometric modalities such as fingerprint acquisition were substan-tially replaced by fully digital verification pipelines [28]. Consequently, identity verification systems increasingly rely on visual inspection of submitted docu-ment images rather than direct access to centralised identity databases [1, 21]. These systems often combine document analysis with face matching between

This dependence on document images makes such systems vulnerable to localised manipulations, in-cluding face replacement, text editing, or both-field field tampering, which can enable identity fraud while remaining visually difficult to detect through manual inspection [22, 29]. Identity documents differ from natural images, as they contain structured lay-outs composed of semantically meaningful fields such as facial photographs, tex-tual identity information, security textures, and machine-readable zones [21,22]. Even small localised modifications, such as altering a date of birth, modifying a document number, or swapping the facial photograph, can compromise sys-tem integrity and lead to financial or regulatory risks [29]. These examples demonstrate how localised alterations affecting specific identity fields can remain visually subtle while significantly impacting the authenticity of the document. In recent years, relatively few publicly accessible studies have specifically targeted identity-document forgery detection. In practice, many industrial iden-tity verification systems rely on proprietary datasets and internally developed detection models that are not publicly released due to privacy, regulatory, and security considerations [8,24]. Since such datasets typically contain sensitive per-sonal identity information, both the training data and the deployed detection pipelines remain closed-source, limiting reproducibility and motivating the need for open, efficient, and domain-specific approaches. At the same time, general image forgery detection has been extensively studied using deep learning–based approaches. Methods such as TruFor [10], UniVAD [9], and MMFusion [23] have demonstrated strong performance in detecting synthetic or manipulated visual content by leveraging transformer architectures, multimodal fusion mechanisms, or anomaly-based localisation strategies. However, these approaches are primar-Abbreviated paper title 3

ily designed and evaluated on natural-image manipulation benchmarks, where the objective is to detect edits such as object insertion, removal, or generative-image synthesis, and are therefore not optimised for the structured and multi-modal characteristics inherent to identity documents. Consequently, their per-formance often degrades when applied directly to real-world identity verification scenarios. To address these challenges, we propose FLiD (Field-Localized Identity Doc-ument Forgery Detection), a lightweight, field-aware forgery detection framework that explicitly models identity documents as structured collections of semantic identity fields. Rather than processing the entire document indiscriminately, our approach focuses on the semantic regions most susceptible to tampering, namely the facial photograph and textual identity fields. A fine-tuned object detection module is first employed to localise and extract these key identity regions, en-abling consistent preprocessing across heterogeneous document layouts and for-mats. The extracted regions are then encoded using a compact feature backbone to generate efficient yet semantically meaningful embeddings. For documents containing simultaneous manipulations across multiple fields, embeddings from both facial and textual regions are jointly processed by a lightweight classifica-tion module. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed pipeline, evaluated via 5-fold stratified cross-validation, achieves AUC(Area Under the Curve) scores of 0.880 ± 0.054 , 0.954 ± 0.016 , and 0.923 ± 0.036 for face-only, text-only, and both-field manipulation scenarios, respectively, while reducing EER(Equal Er-ror Rate) by up to 35 .64 percentage points over the baseline. These gains are achieved with only 191 K trainable parameters and 21 × fewer FLOPs than the baseline, providing an effective and computationally efficient solution for real-world digital identity verification systems.

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Forgery Detection in Digital Identity Documents 

Identity-focused work emphasizes mobile capture conditions and presentation attacks. Studies on physically occluded fake documents [24] and ID-card PAD in remote verification systems [8] highlighted print–scan and replay attacks, but typically reused generic architectures. PSFNet [26] targeted passport se-curity features, while texture-based analyses [25] examined print–scan inconsis-tencies. The Document Liveness Challenge dataset [20] enabled benchmarking under recapture conditions. Further, ICDAR competitions on tampered text [17] and receipt forgery [18] encouraged document-centric evaluation. Learning-based pipelines for smartphone IDs [6] attempted unified modeling but rarely captured cross-field dependencies explicitly. Shi and Jain [21] proposed DocFace , a deep learning framework for matching ID document photographs with live selfie im-ages using partially shared networks and dynamic weight imprinting for shallow datasets. The importance of presentation attack detection has also been high-lighted through benchmarking efforts such as the PAD-IDCard competition [22]. Beyond document-specific studies, Zheng et al. [29] provide a survey of digital image tampering detection methods. Related work has also examined challenges in ID-to-selfie matching [1] and face-based active authentication on mobile de-vices [7, 19], highlighting issues such as illumination variation, motion blur, and pose changes in mobile capture conditions. 

2.2 Generic Image Forgery Detection 

Early modern learning-based detectors focused on low-level inconsistencies across entire images. MVSS-Net [5] demonstrated that multi-view and multi-scale su-pervision improves localization, but incurred high computational cost and re-mained agnostic to semantics. CAT-Net [13] traced compression artifacts left by splicing operations, but was limited to JPEG-style pipelines. PSCC-Net [16] re-fined masks using progressive spatial–channel modeling, yet still relied on dense feature maps. Later, TruFor [10] unified RGB and trace features to predict ma-nipulation masks and reliability maps, while HiFi-Net [11] introduced hierarchi-cal supervision over forgery attributes. DiffForensics [27] incorporated diffusion priors to improve generalization, and FatFormer [15] adapted transformers for synthetic imagery. Despite improved robustness, these methods treat images as homogeneous grids and explain decisions through pixel heatmaps. Further, UniVAD [9] performs training-free anomaly detection by identifying deviations from normal data, but it does not explicitly model intentional, field-specific edits common in identity fraud. 2.3 Structured and Field-Level Forgery 

Document-specific systems such as DCLNet [14] and edge-focused detectors [2] exploited boundaries and region cues. ID-centric PAD systems [8, 24] and pass-port verification networks [26] similarly focused on localized components. How-ever, MMFusion [23] fused heterogeneous cues but still operated over dense spa-tial grids. Existing systems thus encode document structure only implicitly, mo-tivating our explicit field-token representation and cross-field reasoning. 

# 3 Our Methodology 

We propose an identity-aware forgery detection system that focuses on seman-tically meaningful regions of digital identity documents to improve detection reliability and computational efficiency. Our key insight is that structured iden-tity documents require a fundamentally different detection strategy than natural images: rather than learning dense pixel-level representations, it suffices to ex-tract compact features from semantically critical fields. This principle leads to a compositional design, frozen pretrained features, field-level ROI extraction, and lightweight classification, that achieves strong detection with only 191K trainable parameters while ensuring reproducibility and suitability for edge deployment. As shown in Fig. 2, our pipeline begins by localizing key identity fields, specifi-cally the facial image and textual regions, using a fine-tuned YOLOv8 detector trained on manually annotated identity data. This step separates the input iden-tity document into structured components and enables consistent extraction of identity-relevant regions across diverse document layouts. The detected face and text regions are then cropped and passed through a lightweight MobileNetV3 fea-ture encoder to generate compact embedding representations that capture visual characteristics and potential manipulation artifacts. For documents containing manipulations across multiple identity fields, embeddings from both facial and textual regions are concatenated to form a unified multi-field representation, which is subsequently processed using a lightweight neural network classifier. This classifier learns discriminative patterns from identity-specific features and enables effective classification of identity documents as genuine or forged.

3.2 Identity Field Localization Using YOLOv8 

Identity documents exhibit significant structural variability across countries and issuing authorities, with different layouts, field placements, and visual formats. The Fantasy-ID dataset contains identity cards originating from 13 different countries, resulting in substantial variation in the spatial location and appear-ance of identity fields such as facial photographs and textual information. As an initial step, we manually annotated and cropped the face and textual re-gions for all images in the dataset, creating paired full-document and cropped-field versions. These manually prepared crops served two purposes: (i) enabling controlled preliminary experiments to validate the effectiveness of field-level analysis, and (ii) providing supervision data for training an automatic field-localization model. To automate this process, we adopt YOLOv8, a lightweight real-time object detection architecture, for detecting identity-document fields. YOLOv8 was selected due to its stable implementation, strong detection per-formance, and mature pretrained ecosystem. Since field localisation acts only as a preprocessing stage in our pipeline, this model provides reliable region detection without introducing additional architectural complexity. However, direct application of the pretrained YOLOv8 model resulted in inconsistent localization performance due to the domain-specific char-acteristics of identity documents and the variability in document layouts. After fine-tuning, the de-tector is able to reliably identify face and textual regions across heterogeneous document formats, enabling consistent extraction of semantically meaningful identity fields for downstream forgery detection. 

3.3 Feature Encoding using MobileNetV3 

Following semantic region extraction, each detected identity field (face and tex-tual region) is transformed into a compact visual representation using a lightweight convolutional feature encoder. We adopt MobileNetV3-Small pretrained on ImageNet-1K as the backbone due to its favorable trade-off between computational effi-ciency and representational power, making it suitable for deployment in large-scale identity verification systems where inference latency is critical. The Mo-bileNetV3 backbone weights remain frozen during training; only the downstream classification head is optimized. This frozen-backbone design avoids overfitting on limited identity-document data while retaining the rich visual representations learned from large-scale pretraining. Let If and It denote the cropped face and text field images extracted from an identity document. Each cropped region is resized to a fixed spatial resolution of 

224 × 224 and normalized using ImageNet statistics prior to feature extraction. The processed regions are then passed through the MobileNetV3 encoder ϕ(·),producing 576-dimensional field-level embeddings: 

ef = ϕ(If ), et = ϕ(It),

where ef , et ∈ R576 denote the resulting feature vectors obtained from the final global average pooling layer. These embeddings capture identity-relevant visual cues such as facial geometry, skin-texture consistency, character render-ing artifacts, and localized editing inconsistencies that may arise from tamper-ing operations. To ensure efficient training and reproducibility, embeddings are precomputed and stored for all samples, enabling the subsequent classification stage to operate directly on compact feature representations without repeatedly processing raw images. This design reduces the trainable parameter count to only 191K in the classification head, compared to 2.55M trainable parameters required by the baseline approach, significantly accelerating convergence while preserving discriminative information necessary for forgery detection.

# 4 Performance Evaluation 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

All experiments employ a two-stage pipeline. Region-of-interest (ROI) fields are first localized using a fine-tuned YOLOv8 detector, after which 576-dimensional feature embeddings are extracted from the detected regions using a MobileNetV3-Small backbone pretrained on ImageNet-1K with the classification head re-moved. Input crops are resized to 224 × 224 and normalized using standard Ima-geNet statistics. The resulting frozen embeddings are processed by a lightweight neural network classifier for binary forgery detection (bona fide vs. attack). For the face attack scenario, data augmentation is applied to mitigate limited sample size; detailed augmentation strategies are provided in the supplementary mate-rial. The single-modality classifiers employ a five-layer fully connected neural network with ReLU activations and dropout (0.2–0.3), while the both-field at-tack model uses an analogous architecture with an expanded input layer. All models are trained using Adam (learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −4, batch Abbreviated paper title 9

size 32) with binary cross-entropy loss with logits ( BCEWithLogitsLoss ) and class rebalancing via pos_weight . A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (factor 0.5, patience 5) and early stopping (patience 15, maximum 100 epochs) are applied. We employ stratified 5-fold cross-validation with an 80/20 train–test split in each fold. The split ratio is consistent with the protocol used by González and Tapia [8]. To ensure split integrity on this small-scale dataset, we verified that no document-level leakage exists across train and test partitions: the face attack split has zero document overlap. For the both-field attack scenario, 73 identity labels appear in both partitions; however, since the MobileNetV3 backbone is frozen and never updated on training data, we confirm this overlap is benign and does not inflate test performance (see Supplementary for full analysis). Due to the limited dataset size, no separate validation set is used, and all ISO/IEC 30107-3 metrics are computed on unseen test data. Experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.2.0 on an Apple M-series processor. 

Baseline: As our primary baseline, we reimplement the approach of González and Tapia [8], which trains a MobileNetV2 from scratch (random Kaiming initial-ization, no ImageNet pretraining) on full-document images resized to 448 × 448 

with a 2-class softmax classifier head, optimized using Adam (learning rate 

10 −5). For both-field attacks, the baseline employs a two-stage cascade: sepa-rate face and text models are trained independently, and the final bona fide score is computed as min( Pface (Real ), P text (Real )) , classifying the document as forged if either field is flagged. This cascade design follows the paper’s original protocol. All baseline models are trained on the same data splits and evaluated under identical metrics for a fair comparison. 

Evaluation Metrics: To ensure a fair and standards-compliant comparison, we adopt the ISO/IEC 30107-3 [3, 4] PAD evaluation protocol, consistent with González and Tapia [8]. We report the Attack Presentation Classification Error Rate (APCER), which measures the proportion of attack samples incorrectly classified as bona fide, and the Bona Fide Presentation Classification Error Rate (BPCER), which quantifies the proportion of genuine samples misclassified as attacks. From these threshold-dependent measures, we derive the Equal Error Rate (EER), corresponding to the operating point where APCER and BPCER are equal. Additionally, we report BPCER AP values (e.g., BPCER 10 , BPCER 20 ,BPCER 50 ), which measure the false rejection rate of genuine users while fixing APCER to a predefined security level. These metrics collectively characterize the security–usability trade-off of the PAD system under deployment-relevant operating conditions. For completeness, we also report standard decision-level metrics including Accuracy and F1-score, as well as ROC curves and AUC to assess ranking performance across thresholds.

4.7 Limitations and Future Directions. 

FLiD currently relies on visual feature representations to detect inconsistencies within identity-document fields and achieves strong performance on FantasyID (AUC = 0.954 ± 0.016 ). In the current dataset, most text-editing attacks intro-duce subtle but detectable visual artifacts such as font inconsistencies, blending boundaries, or resolution mismatches, which can be effectively captured by frozen ImageNet features without requiring OCR or language-specific priors. This de-sign enables a language-agnostic pipeline that generalises across diverse docu-ment templates and scripts. As generative editing tools continue to improve, however, future manipulations may become visually seamless while remaining semantically inconsistent. An important direction for future research is there-fore multimodal visual–linguistic fusion, where appearance-based embeddings are complemented with OCR-derived semantic signals to detect semantically implausible but visually plausible edits. Beyond binary detection, an additional direction is forgery localisation . Ex-tending FLiD to produce pixel- or patch-level tampering maps would allow the system to highlight where a manipulation occurs within a field, support-ing forensic analysis and human-in-the-loop verification workflows. Complemen-tary to this, integrating model explainability techniques such as Grad-CAM or attention-based saliency visualisations could provide interpretable evidence of why a document is flagged as forged, improving transparency and opera-tional trust in real-world deployments. Finally, while the current evaluation is conducted on FantasyID, one of the most diverse publicly available identity-document forgery benchmarks, future work will extend the evaluation to addi-tional identity-document datasets to further assess cross-domain robustness and generalisation. 

# 5 Conclusion 

This paper introduced FLiD, a lightweight field-localized framework for detecting forgeries in structured digital identity documents. Unlike general-purpose ma-nipulation detectors that analyse entire images, FLiD explicitly models identity documents as structured compositions of semantic fields and performs forgery detection at the field level. By combining identity-field localisation with com-pact feature extraction and a lightweight classifier, the proposed approach effec-tively captures inconsistencies introduced by face and text manipulations while maintaining very low computational complexity. Extensive experiments on the FantasyID benchmark demonstrate that FLiD consistently outperforms existing general-purpose forgery detectors across multiple manipulation scenarios while using significantly fewer parameters and computational operations. These re-sults highlight the importance of domain-aware modelling for identity-document analysis and show that accurate and reliable forgery detection can be achieved with a compact and efficient architecture suitable for real-world digital identity verification systems.
